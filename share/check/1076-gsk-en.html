Japanese Pop · 1975

Ano Hi ni
Kaeritai

あの日にかえりたい

Written & sung by Yumi Arai (荒井由実)
later known as Matsutoya Yumi (松任谷由実)

Before "City Pop" became a global label, Japan had something quieter and sharper. In 1975, a 21-year-old named Yumi Arai wrote a song about nostalgia so precise it hurt.

Fifty years later, dozens of artists—from enka singers to jazz guitarists—still reach for this one first.

The Song

What it is

A quiet plea to return to a moment already gone — the feeling of youth slipping away before you realized it was happening. No drama, just a hollow ache. It works in any language, which is exactly why so many people keep covering it.

#1 Oricon chart peak
35 weeks on chart
615k copies sold

Source: Wikipedia (ja)

Released October 5, 1975 on Toshiba EMI. Written as a theme for a TBS drama called Katei no Himitsu ("Family Secrets") — the lyrics were actually rewritten once, because the first version didn't fit the story.

Wikipedia (ja)

The Recording

Who played on it

The band on this record is essentially half of Japan's future music industry. In 1975 they were all session players nobody outside Tokyo had heard of.

  • Drums Hayashi Tatsuo (林立夫)
  • Bass / guitar Hosono Haruomi (細野晴臣)
  • Guitar Suzuki Shigeru (鈴木茂)
  • Keys / arr. Matsutoya Masataka (松任谷正隆)
  • Chorus Yamamoto Junko (山本潤子)
  • Producer Murai Kunihiko (村井邦彦)

Wikipedia (ja)

During the recording session, drummer Hayashi Tatsuo dropped his stick — and the sound is still in the final master. Arranger Matsutoya liked the take's feel so much he kept it. Years later, Hayashi admitted: "That wasn't dramatic tension. That was a mistake."

Matsutoya later said he still wonders: "If we'd done it again, would it have sold more — or less?"

Cover Versions · Featured

The versions worth finding

This is the part that keeps growing. Every decade, a different generation of Japanese musicians comes back to this song.

2003

Michael Franks

Over the Sky: Yuming International Cover Album

Retitled "Somewhere in the Rain" — an English-language cover by the American jazz-pop songwriter. The only internationally known Western artist to record this song. If you listen to one cover first, make it this one.

Wikipedia (ja)
2002

Ono Lisa (小野リサ)

Queen's Fellows: Yuming 30th Anniversary Cover Album

The Brazilian-Japanese bossa nova singer turns it into something warm and unhurried. One of the most-cited covers in Japan — and easy to love without any context.

Wikipedia (ja)
1999

Moritaka Chisato (森高千里)

Dear Yuming — Arai Yumi / Matsutoya Yumi Cover Collection

A 1990s J-pop star doing a Yuming tribute album — it fits perfectly. Clean, direct, and surprisingly emotional.

Wikipedia (ja)  ·  moritaka-chisato.com
2009

Sakamoto Fuyumi (坂本冬美)

Love Songs — Mata Kimi ni Koishiteru

Japan's top enka (traditional ballad) singer covering 1970s pop — and it works completely. Her phrasing turns every pause into something deliberate.

YouTube  ·  Wikipedia (ja)
2008

Inagaki Jun'ichi (稲垣潤一)

Otoko to Onna — Two Hearts Two Voices · duet with Tsuyuzaki Harume (露崎春女)

Sung as a duet — which changes the meaning entirely. Where the original is a solo ache, this version sounds like two people trying to remember the same thing.

Wikipedia (ja)
2006

Tokunaga Hideaki (徳永英明)

Vocalist 2

His Vocalist series — male singer covering female pop classics — was a phenomenon in mid-2000s Japan. This is one of the standout tracks: his falsetto strips the song down to the bone.

Wikipedia (ja)
2013

Imai Miki (今井美樹)

Dialogue — Miki Imai Sings Yuming Classics

A full album dedicated to Yuming's songbook. Imai is one of Japan's most gifted ballad singers; her version is measured, careful, and deeply felt.

Wikipedia (ja)
2009

Kuwata Keisuke (桑田佳祐)

Showa 83-nendo! Hitori Kohaku Utagassen (live video)

Frontman of Southern All Stars — Japan's equivalent of The Rolling Stones — performing it solo at a year-end tribute concert. Rough and warm.

Hidden Connection

The song that almost was

"Sky Restaurant" (スカイレストラン) — Hi-Fi Set, 1975

The original draft lyrics for this melody were given to another group instead — Hi-Fi Set (ハイ・ファイ・セット), who released them as a separate song called "Sky Restaurant" on November 5, 1975. Same tune, different words.

That song later found a second life when it was sampled by J. Cole on "January 28th" (2014) and by Chris Brown on 11:11 in "Moonlight."
